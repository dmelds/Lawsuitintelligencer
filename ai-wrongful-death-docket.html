The Inventory Nobody Knows How to Price: Inside the AI Wrongful Death Docket

In ten months, AI wrongful death litigation went from a single San Francisco filing to four distinct case fronts and a steady pipeline of new clients. Firms are building inventory against a docket with no public settlement values, no ruling on the merits, and no federal consolidation. Here is how that inventory is being built, and what reprices it.

By David Meldofsky · June 12, 2026 · 11 min read

Updated November 3, 2026, with the docket’s progress since October 21. Earlier updates are in the update log.

The short version

  • The AI wrongful death docket now runs on four fronts: Raine v. OpenAI, the Tumbler Ridge school shooting suits, the FSU shooting case, and the Scott overdose case, with a wave of additional filings in between. All of it is pretrial.
  • A small group of repeat players is building the inventory, and the signing calculus looks nothing like conventional mass tort intake: the chat log replaces both the product ID and much of the causation file.
  • Raine established the discovery playbook, centered on moderation data and internal safety policy documents, and nearly every later complaint patterns on it.
  • The dispositive motion rulings expected in the second half of 2026 are the repricing event. Until they land, every case in inventory is an option on an unresolved question of law.
  • Update, November 3, 2026: Still no ruling. Amended complaints reset OpenAI’s Florida motions, and a new one is due November 24. In California, a court order now governs how each family gets its ChatGPT records.

Mass tort economics usually rest on reference points. A firm signing a talc case or an AFFF case can look at prior verdicts, settlement grids, Daubert history, and MDL structure, and price the inventory accordingly. The AI wrongful death docket has none of that. There is no MDL, no verdict, no settlement program, and no admissibility ruling. What exists is a body of complaints, one answer, and one instructive ruling from an adjacent case. The filing pace keeps accelerating anyway.

That combination, fast-growing inventory against unpriced risk, is the most interesting business story in plaintiff-side law right now. It is worth taking apart.

Update, November 2026: the first rulings slip, the records start moving

Update, November 3, 2026: No court has yet said whether ChatGPT is a product. The October update named two events that would set the first price. Both moved. Amended complaints reset OpenAI’s Florida motions, and its California responses had not reached the public record by November 2.

California: the records order

The state cases move together in JCCP No. 5431 before Judge Ethan P. Schulman. The court ordered a leadership structure on August 4, covered in The Handwritten Line. Discovery opened on September 23. OpenAI’s responses to the complaints on file by that date were due October 30. The court’s public record listed none as of November 2.

On October 30 the court filed an order setting how OpenAI hands over each family’s ChatGPT records. The family’s lawyers identify the account, and OpenAI names the accounts it finds within 15 days. Once the family consents, OpenAI has 15 more days to give counsel 60 days of online access.

The production covers chats, edited prompts, voice transcripts, uploaded files and saved memories. It also covers OpenAI’s own safety and moderation records for each account. This piece calls that document set the discovery playbook. A court order now delivers it case by case.

The court sits again on November 13. Plaintiffs may amend by November 16. Fact sheets are due December 15, and the defendants respond to the amended complaints on December 16. Suits filed after September 23 fall outside the October 30 deadline.

California: new filings, and a fifth front

The pipeline kept filling. On October 14, four new suits were filed in San Francisco Superior Court. The parents of Mathias Habib, a 15-year-old in Lebanon who died in April, say ChatGPT kept talking with their son after he told it he was thinking about suicide. Two survivors say months of ChatGPT use fed their delusions. A fourth plaintiff says ChatGPT helped his estranged wife plan to poison him.

On October 27, the parents of Bowen Wan sued as an add-on to the coordination. Wan was a 31-year-old Seattle engineer with epilepsy who died in February. Her parents say ChatGPT built her a plan to come off her seizure medication without her doctors. One of their eight counts pleads unlicensed practice of medicine and psychology.

Wan carries the Scott overdose theory from a single drug answer to months of medical management. Its licensing count runs on the theory Pennsylvania’s Board of Medicine brought against Character Technologies in May. OpenAI called the death heartbreaking and said the model involved, GPT-4o, is no longer offered.

A different claim arrived on October 13. A mother suing as Jane Doe filed a proposed federal class action in San Francisco over children’s data used to train OpenAI’s models. She asks the court to hold that children are not bound by ChatGPT’s terms, including its arbitration clause. Any inventory holding a minor’s account has a stake in that answer.

Florida: the motions reset

The seven FSU shooting cases run on one schedule under the Joshi case number before Chief Judge Mark E. Walker. Grant, first filed in Leon County, is now handled with the federal cases. In October the court set aside OpenAI’s motions against Mall and Askins, then against Gourley, Morales and Grant, after each plaintiff amended. One new motion covering all five amended complaints is due November 24.

The motion against the Chabba family’s complaint in Joshi is still pending. OpenAI filed it on September 25, and its reply was due November 3. On October 30, the court accepted a brief supporting OpenAI from two technology industry groups.

The motion treats ChatGPT’s output as information, which Florida product law does not reach. It denies that OpenAI owed any duty to a bystander harmed by someone else’s crime. It calls negligent entrustment a poor fit for software and raises the First Amendment. Section 230 is absent from it.

OpenAI also put the shooter’s chat history before the court by sworn declaration and invited the judge to read it. The chat log, which this piece calls the plaintiff’s core artifact, is now the defense’s exhibit too. A ruling in Joshi remains the nearest answer to the question this piece is about.

Tumbler Ridge: one proceeding

On October 28, Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley combined all 38 Tumbler Ridge cases as In re Tumbler Ridge Shooting Litigation, No. 3:26-cv-03701-JSC. The Province of British Columbia’s suit is among them. The next day, the lead April case, the Rushlow case and the province each filed an amended complaint.

On October 26, OpenAI asked the B.C. Supreme Court to halt the provincial privacy commissioner’s investigation of the shooting. The commissioner wants all of the shooter’s ChatGPT logs and OpenAI’s records on why it did not alert police. OpenAI says it gave the logs to police and broke no B.C. privacy rule.

The pricing variables

Character Technologies, the Garcia defendant, is selling itself in Chapter 11. Disney made the opening bid of $20 million, and the sale hearing is set for November 24. That is the solvency pressure this piece says OpenAI does not face.

Texas sued OpenAI and Sam Altman on October 23, the tenth state to sue the company. Florida and Texas ask their courts to approve new models before release. Those suits are tracked in the state AI enforcement tracker.

The thesis stands. Every case in inventory is still an option on an open question of law. The calendar has moved out by a month. Three events now set the first price: the Joshi ruling, OpenAI’s November 24 motion in the other FSU cases, and its December 16 responses in California.

The docket, briefly

Raine v. OpenAI, filed August 26, 2025 in San Francisco County Superior Court, is the foundation. The parents of a 16-year-old who died by suicide sued OpenAI and Sam Altman personally. They pleaded seven causes of action anchored on California strict products liability.

An amended complaint in October 2025 sharpened the theory from rushed-to-market negligence to intentional misconduct. It alleges the deliberate removal of self-harm safeguards, which opens the path to punitive damages through the survival action. OpenAI answered in November, denying responsibility and arguing the harm resulted from misuse in violation of its terms of use.

Around that anchor, the docket widened in waves. November 2025 brought a coordinated set of additional suits in California courts alleging deaths and psychological harm tied to extended ChatGPT use.

Three more fronts opened in a six-week stretch this spring. Seven federal suits over the Tumbler Ridge school shooting were filed in the Northern District of California in late April. The FSU shooting case followed in federal court in Florida in May, with a novel negligent entrustment count. Days later came the Scott overdose case in California state court, alleging the model misinformed a user about a fatal drug combination.

Different harms, same architecture: ChatGPT as a product, safeguards as the defect, internal data as the proof.

The only merits-adjacent signal in the entire space remains Garcia v. Character Technologies. In May 2025, a Florida federal court let product liability claims against a chatbot maker proceed past dismissal. It declined to resolve the First Amendment defense at the pleading stage. One survival of one motion in one adjacent case is, at present, the closest thing this docket has to a comparable. Garcia later settled in principle, as disclosed on January 7, 2026, on terms that are not public.

Who is building the inventory

So far, a small group of repeat players dominates the filings. Edelson PC brought the Tumbler Ridge cases and appears in the Raine matter. The Tech Justice Law Project's involvement runs through the docket's most consequential pleadings. The Social Media Victims Law Center carried its client base and theory-building from the social media addiction litigation directly into the AI cases.

The continuity matters. These firms spent the last three years litigating design-defect theories against platform defendants. They arrived at the AI docket with the doctrinal scaffolding, the expert relationships, and the client pipelines already in place.

For everyone else, the strategic question is the familiar one from every emerging tort. Enter now, while the law is unsettled and the signing criteria are still being invented. Or wait for the dispositive rulings and pay for the delay in referral fees. The difference here is the steepness of the curve. In most emerging torts the science is the gating uncertainty. In this one it is the law itself.

Signing criteria without a diagnosis code

Conventional mass tort intake screens on two artifacts: proof of product use and a qualifying diagnosis. The AI cases compress both into a single artifact that no prior docket has had, the complete conversation history between the product and the person harmed.

That changes the intake calculus in several ways. The chat log is at once the product identification, the exposure history, and the core causation evidence. It sits in the defendant's possession, in a form the plaintiff can usually also export. Screening a case means reading it.

The strongest files in the current docket share a recognizable pattern. They show extended engagement over months and escalating dependence. They show explicit warning signs the system demonstrably registered, and output a jury could read as participation rather than passivity. The weakest files are short, ambiguous, or missing the account access needed to reconstruct the record.

Preservation is the immediate practice point. Account deletion, conversation deletion, and model deprecation all threaten the central evidence. The families who become clients are often the same people most likely to delete an account in grief. Firms working this space have learned to treat data export and litigation hold notices as day-one tasks, ahead of nearly everything else.

Timing carries a damages dimension that has already shaped the docket once. California's Senate Bill 447 window let survival-action plaintiffs recover for a decedent's pre-death pain and suffering in cases filed before January 1, 2026. That window helps explain the density of late-2025 filings. Cases filed after it closed lose that component unless the legislature extends it. Intake teams now have to price that difference in case value by filing date.

The discovery playbook Raine wrote

The Raine amended complaint did more than add a punitive damages theory. It established what discovery in these cases is for. The complaint's evidentiary core is OpenAI's own moderation data: the allegation that the company's systems flagged hundreds of messages for self-harm content without intervention. Detection worked. Response did not.

That gap between what the system saw and what it did is the structural argument. It points discovery at a specific document set. The set holds moderation logs, safety policy documents and their revision history, internal debates over guardrail changes, and the launch-decision record for each model version.

Nearly every complaint filed since asks for some version of the same record. The Tumbler Ridge cases allege internal flagging of the eventual shooter months before the event. The Scott case patterns on the removed-safeguards theory directly. For defendants, this convergence makes the same internal documents relevant across multiple proceedings in multiple courts. That condition has historically produced coordination pressure. In dockets that survive their early motions, it eventually produces settlement pressure.

The consolidation math

There is no MDL and no immediate prospect of one. The docket is split between California state court, where Raine, Scott, and much of the November wave sit, and federal courts in California and Florida.

The California state cases are already coordinated. A February 3, 2026 order gathered them in San Francisco as In re: ChatGPT Product Liability Cases, JCCP No. 5431. That is the structure that organizes the social media addiction cases. The federal cases sit in two courts, each already managing its own group. Judge Corley has the 38 Tumbler Ridge cases, and Chief Judge Walker has the FSU cases. Neither group needs the Panel.

The practical consequence is that the early rulings will come retail, court by court, with all the inconsistency that implies. That is a risk and an opportunity in equal measure. A favorable ruling in one court does not bind the next, but it builds the persuasive record. The firms steering the docket are visibly sequencing their arguments with that in mind.

Pricing the option

Strip away the technology and the inventory question is familiar: what is a contingency case worth when liability law is unresolved? Three features distinguish this docket's version of the problem.

First, the defendant can fund any defense indefinitely. OpenAI's most recent reported valuation puts it among the most valuable private companies in the world. Unlike the asbestos or talc defendants of prior generations, it faces no solvency pressure to resolve anything. Settlement, if it comes, will be a reputational and regulatory calculation, not a balance-sheet one.

Second, the insurance picture is genuinely unclear. Whether existing commercial liability programs respond to AI output claims is an open question being worked out in parallel. The answer shapes settlement capacity and the willingness of carriers to force early resolution. Firms valuing inventory against an uninsured or under-insured liability are making a different bet than the one mass tort economics usually assumes.

Third, the repricing event has a date range. Dispositive motion rulings across the docket were expected in the second half of 2026, and the November update puts the first of them at the turn of the year. Suppose the product framing survives in even one or two courts. The inventory built over the past year converts from speculative to conventional, and the referral market floods. The firms that signed early own the cases everyone else now wants.

Suppose instead the early rulings go the other way. The docket survives, because the theories are too varied and the courts too many for a single ruling to end it. The option premium evaporates, though, and the next round of filings gets much more selective.

Either way, the present moment is the cheap part of the curve. That is what the filing pace is telling you. The firms building this inventory see the uncertainty clearly. They are pricing it, and they have concluded that the cost of being early is lower than the cost of being late.
