The Docket the Panel Wouldn't Build

Twice denied an MDL, the video game addiction litigation did not consolidate and did not die. It re-formed around a California coordination, scattered federal filings, and, since February, government plaintiffs. For plaintiff firms, the structure is now the story.

By David Meldofsky · July 14, 2026 · 8 min read

Updated October 31, 2026, with the docket’s progress since July, and to correct when the Champion school district filed and a case count the piece gave for the California proceeding.

The short version

  • The JPML has refused to centralize video game addiction claims twice: MDL No. 3109 in June 2024 and the narrower "gateway games" petition, MDL No. 3168, in December 2025.
  • The center of gravity is now JCCP No. 5363 in Los Angeles Superior Court, which coordinates cases against Roblox, Epic, Microsoft, and Activision from Los Angeles and at least four other California counties. Some Fortnite cases are in discovery.
  • Judge Lawrence Riff heard the defendants’ free-speech motions to dismiss on October 21, 2026. Two federal judges have already dismissed addiction claims on First Amendment grounds. Under California’s anti-SLAPP statute, a defendant who wins can recover its fees from the family that sued.
  • Individual federal complaints keep landing anyway: S.D.N.Y. in January, Nevada and Pennsylvania in June. A February filing by an Ohio school district marked the arrival of institutional plaintiffs. In September a Los Angeles judge let Los Angeles County’s own suit against Roblox go forward.
  • Arbitration is being decided one defendant and one family at a time. The same Pennsylvania judge sent one family to arbitration in May and kept another in court in September.
  • No federal MDL means no national leadership slate, no common-benefit architecture, and no single bellwether engine. That shapes venue strategy, discovery leverage, and client acquisition in ways the category has not fully priced.

The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation has now told the video game addiction plaintiffs no twice, for two different reasons, and the second no is the more instructive one. Both refusals left the cases to find another structure.

The first motion, docketed as MDL No. 3109, In re: Video Game Addiction Products Liability Litigation, was denied on June 5, 2024. That petition swept in more than thirty defendants across games that only partially overlapped. The panel noted that the civil conspiracy claim was pled against a different group of companies in each suit. The full order is preserved on our sister site’s primary-source page for the MDL No. 3109 order.

Plaintiffs read the denial as a breadth problem and came back narrower. MDL No. 3168, In re: Gateway Video Game Addiction Products Liability Litigation, covered only the three games children most often play first: Roblox, Fortnite, and Minecraft. The panel denied that one too, on December 10, 2025. The renewed motion dropped the allegation that the companies acted in concert, so the panel held plaintiffs to a heavier burden. It also read the unnamed Doe defendants as a sign the case would grow too unwieldy for one judge.

Note what the panel did not say. It did not call the design-defect theory frivolous, and it centralized a Roblox docket on a different theory the same month. The refusals were about shape, not merit. But refusing a shape is still a decision with consequences, because the litigation went and found another one.

The shape it took instead

The center of gravity is a state coordination, not a federal one. The Judicial Council of California approved coordination in May 2025. JCCP No. 5363 sits before Judge Lawrence P. Riff in Los Angeles Superior Court. It gathers suits against companies including Roblox, Epic Games, Microsoft, and Activision, filed in Los Angeles and transferred in from Alameda, Fresno, Kern, and Riverside counties.

The complaints run on a common design grammar: loot boxes, intermittent rewards, virtual currency, engagement loops. They allege the companies deployed it against minors without warning their parents. Some Fortnite cases have entered discovery. No trial date has been set. Whatever record gets built in this litigation is, for now, getting built in Los Angeles.

First the record has to survive the pleadings. Three motions to dismiss are pending, one from Roblox, one from Microsoft, and a joint motion from Epic, Microsoft and Mojang. The First Amendment is the argument doing the work. The court moved the hearing from July 29 to October 21, 2026, and no ruling had been reported as of October 31.

The defendants can point to a federal record. In April 2025 a judge in the Northern District of Illinois dismissed the Angelilli claims against Roblox, Google, and Apple. She held that game design and user content were protected expression or covered by Section 230. In August 2025 a Western District of Missouri judge dismissed Courtright v. Epic Games on First Amendment grounds. He also rejected the failure-to-warn claim, reasoning that a compelled warning about gameplay would compel speech about protected content.

California raises the stakes of that motion for the plaintiffs. The defendants brought the motions under the state’s anti-SLAPP statute. A defendant who wins an anti-SLAPP motion recovers its attorney’s fees from the plaintiff. If Judge Riff follows the federal courts, the families who sued owe the companies’ fees. That exposure follows every California add-on filed before he rules.

Federal filings did not stop; they just stopped aggregating. A 56-page complaint landed in the Southern District of New York on January 23 against Epic, Mojang, and Microsoft. A Northern District of California complaint filed at the end of December leads with neuroimaging research on internet gaming disorder. June brought new federal filings from a Nevada teenager and a Pennsylvania family, each naming the same familiar defendant cluster. Each case proceeds alone, which is exactly the posture the panel chose when it declined to build the container.

The institutional turn

Firms working the social media addiction litigation will recognize the February development. The Champion Local School District filed a federal complaint in the Northern District of Ohio against Roblox, Microsoft, and Mojang. It alleges that the platforms’ engagement design harmed students. The district says it had to divert resources toward counseling, intervention, and classroom management.

That is the school-district playbook from the social media docket, ported to games: an institutional plaintiff with an institutional damages theory. The district never clicked through anyone’s end-user agreement. The terms-of-service and arbitration defenses that framed the early motions against individual players do not reach it.

The district has since amended its complaint to ask for changes to the games themselves for users under 18. It wants daily play limits and lockouts during school hours and overnight, unless a parent overrides them. It also wants Robux and Minecoins removed in favor of dollar prices at purchase. A family suing for damages asks the court for money. Champion asks the court to rewrite the product, which is relief only an institutional plaintiff is positioned to seek.

The second government plaintiff came out of the child-safety track. On September 24, 2026, Judge Dean J. Kitchens of Los Angeles Superior Court refused to dismiss Los Angeles County’s suit against Roblox. He rejected both the Section 230 defense and an anti-SLAPP motion of the kind now pending before Judge Riff.

The County sues over what Roblox told families and how it verified ages. The addiction cases sue over how the games are built, which is the ground the federal judges held protected. The Kitchens ruling shows where the defense line runs. Claims about a company’s statements and safeguards have gotten past it, and claims about game design have not yet.

In the social media litigation, one school district has become hundreds. The defense bar has noticed the difference between defending against families and defending against governmental entities with counsel, budgets, and no arbitration clause in sight. The gaming docket now has a school district and a county, each pleading around the arbitration clauses that have sent individual players out of court.

What the structure means for plaintiff firms

A federal MDL is not just a venue; it is an operating system. It comes with a leadership slate, a common-benefit fund, coordinated science, and a bellwether engine that manufactures the data points settlements are priced against. The panel declined to install that operating system here, twice, so the category runs on whatever the parties assemble themselves.

The 2026 mass tort map describes the plaintiff bar’s options as a barbell. At one end sit the proven mega-dockets, such as talc, AFFF, and hair relaxer, with known injuries and familiar defendants. At the other end sit the new dockets growing fastest, such as social media and Uber passenger assault. Between the two ends sits a thin middle of theories the JPML has refused to centralize, and video game addiction is one of them.

The firms in the middle are improvising the infrastructure an MDL would have provided. Three practical consequences follow.

The first is venue. Every new case forces a choice of court. A case filed in California joins the JCCP, where the discovery and the expert work are shared across every coordinated case. It also faces the same free-speech argument the defendants made on October 21. If Judge Riff accepts that argument, the family can owe the defendants’ attorney’s fees.

A case filed on its own in federal court keeps control of its schedule and goes before a federal jury pool. The firm also carries every cost alone, and the two federal judges who have ruled on the free-speech defense so far sided with the defendants. The right answer varies case by case.

The second is discovery leverage, which concentrates in Los Angeles. The JCCP record will do the work bellwethers usually do, on a slower and less legible clock.

The third is arbitration, which gets relitigated defendant by defendant instead of being resolved once for the docket. In May 2026, Judge Mia Roberts Perez of the Eastern District of Pennsylvania sent Tomlin v. Epic Games to arbitration on the family’s acceptance of the terms. In September she kept Mercado v. Roblox in court. She found the child had accepted Roblox’s terms, but the child had filed a declaration disaffirming them, which the law lets a minor do.

On September 3, 2026, a divided Ninth Circuit panel held in an unpublished ruling that Roblox waived arbitration by litigating for eleven months first. Every intake now needs the same facts: who accepted the terms, whether the minor disaffirmed them, and how long the defendant litigated before moving to compel. In the Roblox child-safety MDL, Judge Seeborg will hear arbitration test cases from Roblox and Discord on January 13, 2027.

A fourth consequence is quieter. Without an MDL, there is no anchor docket doing the passive marketing that a captioned, numbered, nationally reported proceeding does on its own. Consumer demand in this category is real and searchable, but it has to be met deliberately. The consumer-side coverage at Lawsuit Informer tracks the cases in plain English.

Lawsuit Center runs the intake side, with a video game lawyers guide and a case review pipeline. Both were built for this decentralized posture, where no JPML caption explains the litigation to anyone. Firms running their own intake in the category face the same compliance environment we mapped in the intake analysis, with none of the MDL-era shortcuts.

The panel was asked to build a container and declined. The claims are being litigated anyway, in a structure nobody designed, and structures nobody designed are where the interesting mistakes get made. That is worth watching whether or not you have a single case in the category.

The claims described in this article are allegations by the parties and have not been adjudicated, and the defendant companies dispute them. Case status is current as of the October 31, 2026 update; the consumer-facing tracker at Lawsuit Informer is updated as the dockets move.
