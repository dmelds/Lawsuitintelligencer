The Qui Tam Bill That Can’t Stand Alone

S. 5183 would let private relators sue in the name of the United States to claw back the proceeds of executive-branch self-dealing. The headline number circulating online is copied from existing law. The provision worth reading twice is the one defining who gets to intervene.

By David Meldofsky · August 4, 2026 · 7 min read

The short version

  • S. 5183, the Anti-Corruption Bureau Creation Act, was introduced July 30 by Senator Schumer with Senators Kim, Merkley, and Padilla, and referred to the Committee on Finance.
  • Title I creates a False Claims Act-style private right of action against a defined class of federal officials, their close family, campaign officers, and contractors.
  • The relator award widely reported as a novel "up to 30 percent" is the same band Congress wrote into 31 U.S.C. § 3730(d) decades ago.
  • Title I defines the intervening "Government" as the Anti-Corruption Bureau, an agency that does not exist unless Titles II and III survive. Severability in § 106 does not solve that.
  • The bill has no realistic path in this Congress. Its drafting choices are the reason to read it anyway.

A clip circulated over the weekend claiming that Senate Democrats had introduced a bill letting any American sue the President’s family and keep up to thirty percent of the recovery. The bill is real. The number is real. What almost none of the coverage mentions is that the number was already law.

S. 5183, the Anti-Corruption Bureau Creation Act, was introduced on July 30 by Senator Chuck Schumer for himself and Senators Andy Kim, Jeff Merkley, and Alex Padilla, and referred to the Committee on Finance. The full text was printed in the Congressional Record at pages S4371 through S4385. It runs five titles. Title I is the part that concerns anyone who litigates qui tam.

The award bands are borrowed, not invented

Section 105 sets the relator’s share. It runs three subsections, and it is short enough to read in full.

S. 5183, 119th Cong. § 105 (2026)

Sec. 105. Award to Plaintiffs.

(a) Government Proceeds With Action.—If the Government proceeds with an action brought by a person under section 104, the court shall award the person not less than 15 percent and not more than 25 percent of the proceeds of the action or settlement of the action, depending upon the extent to which the person substantially contributed to the prosecution of the action.

(b) Cases in Which Government Does Not Proceed.—If the Government does not proceed with an action brought by a person under section 104, the court shall award the person not less than 25 percent and not more than 30 percent of the proceeds of the action or settlement of the action.

(c) Fees, Expenses, and Costs.—The court shall award the reasonable attorneys’ fees, expenses, and costs of the person bringing the action under section 104, which shall be paid out of the proceeds of the action or settlement of the action prior to any distribution to the United States Government.

Anyone who has filed under the False Claims Act will recognize subsections (a) and (b) immediately.

Relator award structure compared
Posture S. 5183 § 105 31 U.S.C. § 3730(d)
Government intervenes 15 to 25 percent 15 to 25 percent
Government declines 25 to 30 percent 25 to 30 percent
Fees and costs Paid from proceeds before distribution to the United States Recoverable from the defendant

The bands are lifted wholesale. The fee mechanism is not: under the False Claims Act a successful relator recovers fees and expenses from the defendant, while § 105(c) takes them off the top of the recovery, ahead of the public fisc. In a large case that is a meaningful difference in who bears the cost of the litigation, and it is the one genuinely novel economic term in the section. It went unmentioned in every piece of coverage I read.

What Title I actually reaches

Section 102 defines the covered class broadly. It includes the President and Vice President, their spouses and adult children, and the spouses of those adult children. It extends to Executive Schedule level I officials, Executive Office of the President staff paid at level II or above, special government employees, senior Justice Department officials, the Director of Central Intelligence, the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, the chair and treasurer of the President’s principal campaign committee, and contractors as defined in 41 U.S.C. § 7101. A one-year tail follows anyone out of office.

Section 103 makes it unlawful for a covered person, in connection with the receipt, use, or control of public funds, property, or authority, to corruptly use that office for personal enrichment above an aggregate threshold of $50,000. Liability runs to a civil penalty of not less than $50,000 per violation, disgorgement with prejudgment interest, and treble the Government’s damages. Subsection (c) is the operative multiplier: each transaction or course of conduct through which a covered person obtains personal enrichment is a separate violation. Penalties stack per deal.

Section 104 is False Claims Act procedure with the serial numbers barely filed off. Complaint served on the Government with substantially all material evidence, filed in camera, sealed no fewer than sixty days, defendant not served until the court orders it, sixty-day intervention window with good-cause extensions, first-to-file bar, voluntary dismissal only on written consent. The limitations period is ten years, and § 104(g)(2) expressly reaches conduct occurring on or after January 20, 2025. The frivolous-suit guardrail in § 104(h) is narrower than the sponsors’ summary suggests: fees shift to a prevailing defendant only where the claim was clearly frivolous, clearly vexatious, and brought primarily for harassment. Conjunctive, in a declined case only.

The provision that decides the rest

Sections 104(i) and 105(d) define “Government,” for purposes of intervention, election, motion, and consent, to mean the Bureau acting through its general counsel. Not the Attorney General. Not the Justice Department. The Anti-Corruption Bureau established under Title II.

That single definitional choice makes Title I dependent on the constitutional durability of Titles II through IV. The sealed complaint has to be served on someone. The sixty-day clock has to run against someone. Dismissal requires the consent of a Chair who exists only if the Bureau does. If the Bureau falls, the private right of action is not merely weakened, it is missing the party the statute assigns to receive, evaluate, and elect on every case filed under it.

Section 106 supplies severability for Title I. Severability preserves the title against invalidation of its own provisions. It does not manufacture an intervenor.

That matters more than usual here because the sponsors know exactly which way the wind is blowing, and drafted into it. The findings at § 3(a)(18) cite Trump v. Slaughter and Trump v. Cook, both decided June 29, 2026, alongside Buckley v. Valeo, for the proposition that Congress may create independent agencies that are independent in fact and appearance with functions essentially of an investigative and informative nature. Section 3(b)(6) then states, as a statutory purpose, the creation of an establishment fitting that description.

Read Title III against that sentence. The Bureau receives exclusive jurisdiction over civil enforcement of the Federal Election Campaign Act, chapters 95 and 96 of the Internal Revenue Code, and the government ethics provisions of title 5. It gets subpoena power, authority to impose administrative fines, authority to order disgorgement, divestiture, and recusal, and authority to bring civil actions in district court to enforce those orders. Section 301(h) attaches criminal penalties of up to five years to executive-branch attempts to direct the Bureau on partisan grounds.

Whether that is an entity whose functions are essentially investigative and informative is the central question the bill poses, and the bill poses it against itself. The findings claim the safe harbor; the operative text builds an enforcement agency. Title IV compounds the problem by creating a three-judge division of the D.C. Circuit empowered to appoint temporary Bureau members, who must be retired federal judges, whenever a vacancy goes unfilled for fourteen days, or when the President fails to nominate initial members within 180 days. That is the Morrison v. Olson architecture, deployed to fill seats on a multi-member enforcement body rather than to appoint a single inferior officer prosecutor, and deployed in a doctrinal climate considerably less hospitable to it than 1988.

None of this is hidden. It is on the face of the text, in the sponsors’ own findings, which is what makes the drafting interesting rather than careless.

Read the findings as advocacy

Section 101 recites at length what the sponsors characterize as self-dealing by the current administration, with dollar figures attached to cryptocurrency ventures, a Kazakhstan mining transaction, defense technology holdings, and named donors. Those are the sponsors’ allegations, drafted for a press conference as much as for a court, and several are sourced in the text to news reporting rather than to any adjudicated record. They belong in a piece about this bill as characterizations, not as findings of fact. Statutory findings are argument in statutory clothing, and this set is unusually candid about it.

The same discipline applies to the retroactivity provision. A ten-year limitations period reaching conduct back to January 20, 2025 invites the obvious challenge, and civil penalty statutes have survived worse. But treble damages plus stacked minimum penalties plus disgorgement, applied retroactively to conduct that was not actionable under this section when it occurred, is a punitive-character argument a defendant will make on day one.

Why read a bill that dies

S. 5183 is not going to a markup, let alone a floor. Republicans hold the Senate. The referral to Finance rather than to Judiciary or Rules is itself a signal about how seriously the calendar will take it.

It is still worth the hour, for three reasons. First, this is the most fully drafted attempt anyone has made to extend the qui tam device beyond procurement fraud into political corruption, and drafted text has a way of resurfacing. State legislatures borrow federal drafts. So do later Congresses, in better weather.

Second, the relator economics in § 105(c) are a live idea independent of this bill. Fees paid off the top of the recovery rather than shifted to the defendant changes the risk calculus for firms evaluating contingent public-recovery work, and it will show up again.

Third, and most immediately: the qui tam device itself is under constitutional attack right now in the False Claims Act context, on Article II grounds. A bill that proposes to expand relator standing while its own findings acknowledge the Court’s recent separation-of-powers work is a useful document for anyone tracking where that fight goes. The sponsors built their agency around what they read the Court to have permitted. Whether they read it correctly is the question the next round of qui tam litigation is going to answer regardless of what happens to S. 5183.
